2024 – presentRegulated medical deviceClinical investigation

A clinical vital-signs monitoring device

Taking a research prototype and turning it into medical device software a regulator would accept — while it was still being actively developed.

The device measures vital signs from ear-based sensors. When I picked it up it was research code: it worked, in the sense that it produced numbers, but nothing about it was ready for use in a clinical study. My job was the transition — getting it to a state where it could run a study supporting a UK regulatory submission, and where the software's behaviour was documented and defensible rather than just observed.

That meant two kinds of work at once. The engineering: making the data pipeline reliable enough to run continuously without losing frames, and building the on-device display that surfaces readings and warnings to whoever is running the session. And the regulatory: the development plan, the risk analysis, and the paper trail connecting every requirement to a test that proves it was met.

What I owned

  • The software transition — from research code to software that meets medical device requirements, on a device that was still changing underneath me.
  • The real-time pipeline — continuous capture and analysis running in parallel, restructured so the two never contend with each other.
  • The regulatory documentation set — development plan, procedures, risk analysis, configuration management, and requirements traceability through to verification.
  • Requirements engineering across a team spanning clinical, hardware and software backgrounds, none of whom shared a vocabulary at the start.
  • Field support — diagnosing and fixing issues on devices already deployed, under change control.

What it took

Mostly patience with two incompatible pressures. Regulated development wants everything specified before it's built; an early-stage device wants to keep changing. Getting both meant being deliberate about what was frozen and what wasn't, and being honest with the team about which was which.

Standards

IEC 62304 and ISO 13485, with risk analysis under ISO 14971. I owned the software side of all three.

2024 – presentConsumer productShown at CES 2026

An assistive gaming controller

A device that lets you trigger a game action with an ear movement — built for streamers, and for players who can't reliably use their hands.

This is the commercial side of the business, and I'm the only software engineer on it. That means the whole stack is mine: the firmware running on the custom board, the protocol it speaks to a computer, the Windows application most people will use, and the Android app for playing on a phone or tablet.

The interesting constraint is that the product only works if it feels instant and never misfires. An assistive input device that occasionally misses is worse than no device — the user has already been let down by enough technology. Most of the engineering effort goes into that reliability rather than into features.

What I owned

  • Firmware for the custom board — start-up, storage, communication with the host, and the factory provisioning process for new units.
  • The Windows application — architecture designed from scratch to absorb changing requirements without rewrites, plus detection, per-user calibration, and the release and packaging process.
  • The Android application — from product spec through architecture and build to preparing it for store release, including the accessibility permissions case the product depends on.
  • Consistency across platforms — making sure the device behaves identically whether it's plugged into a PC or a phone, which turned out to be harder than building either.
  • Release engineering — automated builds and checks, versioned artifacts, and a written release procedure so shipping isn't a memory exercise.
  • User testing with domain consultants, and prioritising the interface work off the back of it.

What it took

Deciding what not to build. Early on the product vision included things the platforms simply don't allow. I brought a prioritised breakdown to the people who owned that vision, was explicit about which requirements weren't achievable and why, and proposed alternatives — so the schedule was built on what was actually possible.

Sole engineer

No separate applications team to hand a layer to. Every decision from the board's message format to the installer was one person's.

2023 – 2024R&D£1.3M UKRI-funded

The research platform both products came from

A funded research programme extracting health metrics from ear-based sensors — and the prototype software that made the results visible to everyone else.

I was the software specialist on a Healthy Ageing research project, working alongside clinicians and hardware engineers. The output was a working proof of concept: an application that pulled live sensor data, processed it, and showed the results in a way a non-engineer could interpret and challenge. Alongside it, cross-platform tools the wider team used to validate sensor data over different connection types.

I also put the basic engineering discipline in place — version control practice and written procedures for a team where most people hadn't worked that way before. Unglamorous, and the reason the later regulated work was possible at all.

The lesson I took from it

I chose a heavy web-based framework for the prototype interface. It was the right call for a fast, polished demo on a laptop, and the wrong call once the target moved to constrained hardware — the processing core ported cleanly, the interface had to be rebuilt from scratch.

The honest lesson is upstream of the rebuild: on a project with that many unknowns, I should have probed where the software would eventually have to run before committing to a framework. It changed how I make stack decisions now — keep the tested core independent of the interface, and ask about the hardware target before the demo target.

Problems I've solved

Three that were worth the time

Short versions. Happy to go into any of them properly in a conversation.

The chip that hijacked its own bus

Programming new units kept failing partway through, at a different point each time. Nine plausible explanations were tested and eliminated before the real one surfaced: a neighbouring chip was quietly taking over the shared connection about a second after power-up, regardless of what anyone else was doing with it.

The workaround got production moving the same week; the permanent fix went into the next hardware revision.

The test that found it was the one that observed the system directly instead of reasoning about it. It should have run first, not ninth — that's genuinely changed how I sequence a diagnosis.

The clock that lied

Devices already deployed for a clinical study were intermittently losing track of time — about the worst place for a fault that undermines the data record. The system reported the clock as healthy throughout.

The hardware had a warning signal designed for exactly this condition, and the software layer above it was swallowing that warning. Root cause was mundane; the fix went out to deployed units, along with a written analysis and a test procedure so it's now a checked condition rather than something one person happens to remember.

A component reporting itself healthy is not the same as a component being healthy. The work wasn't the fix — it was making an invisible failure visible.

Two platforms, one device, different behaviour

The same detection logic was noticeably more sensitive on mobile than on desktop. A user plugging the device into a phone was effectively getting a different product.

Structured root-cause work under deadline pressure traced it back to an optimisation I'd made myself, which had raised the noise level in the data without anyone noticing. Corrected, and the two brought back in line.

The cause being my own change is the point. Looking there first is faster than looking there ninth.

Process on a product that didn't require any

The regulated programme has standards forcing discipline on it. The consumer programme had nothing forcing anything — which is exactly how an early-stage product becomes an unmaintainable prototype that ships anyway.

I set up build automation, a release procedure, interface documentation and recorded design decisions for the commercial software deliberately: borrowing the parts of regulated practice that earn their keep, and leaving out the parts that only exist to satisfy an auditor.

Working under a formal standard teaches you which process is load-bearing and which is theatre. The goal is software that someone who isn't me can maintain.

How I work

Say it early, in a form people can act on

When a system's requirements outrun what the platform can actually do, I say so early rather than quietly trying to make it work.

That sounds obvious and mostly isn't done, because the early conversation is the uncomfortable one. But a scope problem raised in month one is a planning decision; the same problem raised in month six is a failure. I'd rather bring a prioritised breakdown and a concrete alternative than deliver a surprise later.

The same instinct shows up in debugging: name the discrepancy as soon as you see it, find the mechanism, and don't ship two things that behave differently while hoping nobody notices.

In practice

Both product programmes have had a moment where the honest answer was "not in this timeline, and here's what we can do instead." Both went better for it.

About

Instrumentation, broadly defined

The through-line across everything above is finding hidden or ambiguous system state and making it legible.

A signal buried in raw sensor data. A chip quietly taking over a connection it was supposed to share. A fault that reads as healthy while producing invalid results. The job is always the same shape: find the invisible state, prove what it actually is, build something reliable on top of it — then write it down so the next person doesn't have to rediscover it.
